Why premiums change

What drives insurance premiums?

Premiums start from the expected cost of claims, then add the cost of running an insurer and holding capital. This page sets out each driver and the evidence reported by regulators and industry bodies.

The pricing framework

How actuaries price insurance

Expected claims How often claims occur and what they cost + Expenses Administration, distribution, compliance + Risk and capital loading Reinsurance and the cost of capital = Technical premium The actuarially calculated price

The technical premium

The technical premium is what an insurer needs to charge, on average, to pay expected claims and costs and earn the return its capital requires. It is estimated separately for each risk, using data on location, property or vehicle features and claims history.

The market premium

The price a customer pays can differ from the technical premium. It also reflects competition, the insurer’s pricing strategy and customer behaviour. Taxes and levies, such as stamp duty and GST, are added on top.

Six drivers

What can push premiums up

01

Claims costs

Rebuilding, repair, labour, materials, vehicle parts and replacement values. These determine the cost of each claim.

Evidence. ABS prices for new dwellings rose 41.3% and for vehicle maintenance and repair 27.4% over the five years to the September quarter 2026. Insurers told the ACCC that building material and labour cost inflation contributes to high premiums (ACCC 2025). The ACCC’s final report found claims cost inflation is pushing premiums higher across Australia (ACCC 2026).

02

Natural disasters and climate risk

Floods, bushfires, cyclones and storms, and how insurers assess hazard, exposure and vulnerability at each location.

Evidence. The 2022 flood events produced $7.17 billion in insured losses from more than 300,000 claims (Insurance Council of Australia 2023). How disasters affect premiums

03

Reinsurance

Insurers transfer part of their catastrophe risk to reinsurers. The cost of reinsurance is part of the premium.

Evidence. After the cyclone reinsurance pool lowered reinsurance costs, average home insurance premiums per $100,000 sum insured in higher cyclone risk areas fell 11% in the first year. In areas with no cyclone risk they rose 6%. After up to two years they were 14% below pre-pool levels (ACCC 2026). The Actuaries Institute found that home insurance premium increases in the year to March 2024 were primarily a consequence of higher reinsurance costs during 2023 (Actuaries Institute 2024).

04

Operating and capital costs

Administration, distribution, regulation, and the return investors require on the capital insurers must hold.

Evidence. The cost of capital is part of the cost of insurance. Insurers also pay expenses, commissions, taxes and levies, and often pass these costs on to policyholders (Huang 2024).

05

Competition and pricing strategy

Market conditions, the insurance cycle, customer behaviour and practices such as price optimisation.

Evidence. 31% of car insurance customers who contacted their insurer at renewal obtained a lower premium for the same cover (ASIC 2026). Price optimisation is legal in Australia (Huang 2024).

06

Data and risk modelling

The data and models insurers use to estimate each customer’s risk, including AI and big data.

Evidence. The ACCC found that better access to data, and more sophisticated analysis of it, has enabled insurers in northern Australia to price risk at an individual address level, not at a postcode or regional level, particularly for cyclone and flood risk. This has resulted in very large premium increases for some customers, while lower-risk customers may have seen reductions (ACCC 2020, ix). More individualised pricing can lead to higher premiums for properties with higher risk levels (Huang 2024).

The evidence

What the data show

  • Insurance prices rose 47.0% over the five years to the September quarter 2026, against 24.6% for the all-groups CPI (Trends).
  • Over the same period, ABS prices for new dwellings rose 41.3% and for vehicle maintenance and repair 27.4%.
  • The 2022 flood events produced $7.17 billion in insured losses (Insurance Council of Australia 2023).
  • After the cyclone reinsurance pool lowered reinsurance costs, home premiums in higher cyclone risk areas fell relative to areas with no cyclone risk (ACCC 2026).
