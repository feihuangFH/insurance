Why are some households underinsured or uninsured?

Affordability stress, underinsurance and non-insurance are different measures. What the latest estimates show.

Short answer

A household is underinsured when its cover would not pay the full cost of rebuilding or replacing what it owns. It is uninsured when it has no cover at all.

APRA estimates that around one in seven households in freestanding houses are uninsured today. About half of households surveyed by the ACCC rated their home insurance as unaffordable or barely affordable.

Four different measures

Each measure uses its own data and definition.
Term Meaning Typical measure
Affordability stress Premiums are high relative to income Premium above a set share of household income
Underinsurance Cover is too low to pay for a full rebuild or replacement Sum insured below estimated rebuilding cost
Non-insurance No cover at all Share of households or houses without a policy
Protection gap Losses that insurance does not pay Share of losses not covered by insurance

What the estimates show

Affordability stress. The Actuaries Institute estimates that 15% of Australian households, or 1.61 million, were in home insurance affordability stress in the year to March 2024, up from 12% a year earlier. It defines stress as a home insurance premium above four weeks of gross household income1.

How households rate the cost. In a survey commissioned for its final insurance monitoring report, the ACCC found that around half of the households surveyed across Australia rated their home insurance as unaffordable or barely affordable, regardless of the cyclone risk they face2.

Non-insurance. APRA estimates that around one in seven households in existing freestanding houses are uninsured today. The estimate excludes strata properties such as units and apartments. APRA did not count uninsured households directly. It assumed that a household is likely to go without insurance when its premium is above four weeks of household income. The estimate comes from APRA’s climate stress test, carried out with five large insurers that together write around 80% of home insurance by gross written premium3.

The Australia Institute estimates that in 2023, 344,523 Australian households had no home building insurance and 533,229 were underinsured, about 1 in 30 and 1 in 20 households4. APRA’s estimate covers only freestanding houses and is based on an affordability threshold. The two use different data and definitions and are not directly comparable.

Projections. Under both of APRA’s climate stress scenarios, the share of uninsured households could rise to around one in four by 2050. That is about one million more households than today. By 2050, over 30% of households in regional centres and over 40% in rural areas may be uninsured. APRA states that these are stress scenarios, not forecasts3.

Reported reasons

The ACCC reported hearing that households and small businesses were increasing their excesses and reducing their cover in response to high premiums5.

Home building cover pays up to the sum insured on the policy. Moneysmart lists rising building costs and inflation among the common causes of underinsurance, and suggests checking cover every year6. ABS prices for building new homes, which exclude land, rose 41.3% over the five years to the September quarter 20267.

Consequences

APRA notes that a growing protection gap can lead to greater uninsured losses for households and banks, and higher fiscal costs for governments3.
