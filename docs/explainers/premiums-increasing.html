Why are insurance premiums increasing in Australia?

What the price data show, and the cost pressures reported by regulators and industry bodies.

Short answer

Insurance prices in Australia rose 47.0% over the five years to the September quarter 2026, compared with 24.6% for overall consumer prices.

Regulators and industry bodies report higher claims costs, large natural disaster losses and higher reinsurance costs as pressures on premiums.

How much have premiums risen?

The ABS insurance price index rose 5.4% in the year to September 2026, against 4.3% for the all-groups CPI. Over five years, insurance prices rose 47.0% and all goods and services 24.6% (Insurance trends).

Figures for specific products come from different sources and measure different things.

  • Car insurance. ASIC reports that motor vehicle premiums grew by more than 42% between 2019 and 2024, and by a further 8% in the 12 months to July 2025 (ASIC 2026).
  • Home and contents insurance. The ACCC found that average home and contents premiums outside northern Australia rose 18% in 2023–24 (ACCC 2025), and a further 10%, to $2,310, in 2024–25 (ACCC 2026).

The ABS index tracks the price of comparable policies. Average premiums also change when people insure more valuable homes or change their cover, so the two are not directly comparable.

What goes into a premium?

Actuaries start from a technical premium:

Technical premium = expected claims + expenses + risk and capital loading

Expected claims depend on how often claims happen and how much each one costs. Expenses cover administration, distribution and regulation. The risk and capital loading pays for reinsurance and for the capital an insurer must hold.

The price a customer pays also reflects competition, the insurer’s pricing strategy, and taxes and levies such as stamp duty and GST.

What regulators and industry bodies report

Claims costs

ABS prices for new dwellings rose 41.3% over the five years to the September quarter 2026, and prices for vehicle maintenance and repair rose 27.4%. Insurers told the ACCC that building material and labour cost inflation is one of the factors behind high premiums (ACCC 2025). The ACCC’s final report found that claims cost inflation and extreme weather events are pushing premiums higher across Australia (ACCC 2026).

Natural disasters

The Insurance Council of Australia reported $7.17 billion in insured losses from more than 300,000 claims across the 2022 flood events (Insurance Council of Australia 2023). See How do natural disasters affect insurance premiums?

Reinsurance

The Actuaries Institute found that home insurance premium increases in the year to March 2024 were primarily a consequence of higher reinsurance costs during 2023 (Actuaries Institute 2024).

After insurers joined the government-backed cyclone reinsurance pool, the ACCC found that average home insurance premiums per $100,000 sum insured in higher cyclone risk areas fell by 11% in the first year. In areas with no cyclone risk they rose 6%. Up to two years after insurers joined, premiums in higher-risk areas were 14% below pre-pool levels. Insurers are passing on the lower reinsurance rates to policyholders in higher cyclone risk areas (ACCC 2026).

Capital, taxes and levies

Insurers must hold capital against the risk of large losses, and the cost of that capital is part of the cost of insurance. Insurers also pay expenses, commissions, taxes and levies, and often pass these costs on to policyholders (Huang 2024).

Competition and pricing strategy

ASIC found that 31% of car insurance customers who contacted their insurer about a renewal obtained a lower premium without changing their cover (ASIC 2026). Pricing that takes account of how likely a customer is to shop around, known as price optimisation, is legal in Australia (Huang 2024).

ABS figures: ABS, Consumer Price Index, Australia (Data API, dataflow CPI), original series, retrieved 3 November 2026. Last reviewed 3 November 2026.
